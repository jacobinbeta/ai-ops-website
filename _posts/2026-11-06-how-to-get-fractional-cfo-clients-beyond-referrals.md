Most Fractional CFO practices are built on referrals. Ours was too. I was the founding employee at a Fractional CFO firm and helped grow it from 8 clients to more than 30, and nearly all of them came through referrals.

The problem with referrals isn't quality. It's that you can't schedule them. They arrive in waves, and they stay inside your network.

Some of our best work started a different way. We heard that a company had just opened a new location, so we reached out. They were in the middle of negotiating debt agreements and the build-out terms for the new site, and they needed help. They became a multi-year client.

That client came from noticing the right moment. This article is the system for doing that on purpose: five signals, the exact search for each, what to say, how to follow up, and a weekly routine to keep it going. Everything here is free.

## What makes a good buying signal

A buying signal is an event in a business that creates an immediate need for CFO-level work. A useful one has three traits:

- **It creates a real finance problem,** like new covenants, a board to report to, or a team with no one setting direction.
- **It's public,** so you can find it without inside information.
- **It's recent.** A signal from last week is a reason to talk. A signal from last year is just a fact.

Reaching out on a signal is different from cold outreach. You aren't guessing whether someone might need help someday. You're responding to something that just happened in their business.

## The five signals, and exactly where to find them

**Short on time?** Start with signal 1. It's the clearest sign of need, and the search takes two minutes.

### 1. Hiring a controller with no CFO above them

**Why it matters:** The company has outgrown its bookkeeper and is building a finance team, but nobody sits above the controller. A controller closes the books. They don't build the forecast or manage the bank.

**Where to find it:** On LinkedIn Jobs or Indeed, search your area for postings from the last 30 days:

- `controller "reports to the CEO"`
- `controller "reports to the owner"`
- `controller "reports to the President"`

To catch postings on company careers pages, search Google for `site:boards.greenhouse.io OR site:jobs.lever.co controller "reports to the CEO"`. Save each search and turn on job alerts so new postings come to you.

**First message:**

> Hi [Name], I saw [Company] is hiring a controller who'll report to you. A good controller will tighten up the close. If you'd also like help with the forward-looking side, like cash forecasting and the bank, that's what my firm does part-time. Open to a 20-minute call?

### 2. Searching for a finance leader

**Why it matters:** A company posting for a CFO, VP of Finance or Head of Finance has a gap right now, especially if they're replacing someone who left. A full-time search takes months, and the close and the bank won't wait.

**Where to find it:** Search your area for `"Head of Finance" OR "Director of Finance" OR "VP Finance"`. Prioritize postings that have been open 30 days or more. A long search means they're feeling the gap.

**First message:**

> Hi [Name], I noticed [Company] is searching for a finance leader. While you find the right person, I can step in as fractional CFO so nothing slips. Some companies find part-time covers what they need for now. Worth a quick conversation?

### 3. Opening a new location

**Why it matters:** A new site usually means new debt, lease and build-out terms to negotiate, new tax obligations, and an owner asking whether each location really makes money. That's exactly how the multi-year client above started.

**Where to find it:** Set up two Google Alerts once (google.com/alerts), replacing [your state] with your market:

- `"new location" OR "grand opening" [your state]`
- `"breaks ground" OR "expansion" [your state]`

Also watch for the same company posting jobs in a new city.

**First message:**

> Hi [Name], I saw [Company] is opening in [City]. Expansions like this usually come with new financing, lease and build-out terms to negotiate. I help owners get those right and see how each location performs. Would it help to talk?

### 4. Taking on new debt

**Why it matters:** Lenders bring covenants and regular reporting. Many owners sign the loan and only then learn what the bank expects to receive.

**Where to find it:** The SBA publishes its 7(a) and 504 loan data free on data.sba.gov, including the borrower, city, lender, amount and approval date. Download the latest file, filter to your state and the loan sizes that match your clients, and sort by approval date. The data is published with a lag, so treat it as a "recently financed" list rather than breaking news.

Also ask the commercial bankers you know. They see this signal first, and they want their borrowers to have good financial reporting.

**First message:**

> Hi [Name], I saw [Company] recently closed financing with [Lender]. Banks often ask for more than owners expect, like covenant calculations and borrowing-base reports. If that's landing on your desk, I can take it off your plate.

### 5. Raising outside capital

**Why it matters:** New investors expect board reporting and a credible forecast, often within the first quarter.

**Where to find it:** Companies that raise private money file a Form D with the SEC, usually within 15 days of the first sale. It's free to search and lists the amount, the date and the executives.

1. Go to sec.gov/edgar/search.
2. Enter an industry keyword, set the form type to D, choose the last 30 days, and set "Principal executive offices in" to your state.
3. Skip funds and real estate vehicles. Focus on operating companies.

**First message:**

> Hi [Name], I saw [Company] recently closed a round. The first board meeting after a raise is often when founders feel the reporting gap most. Happy to share the reporting package and forecast structure that works for companies at your stage.

## Qualify each company in two minutes

Before you write to anyone, check three things:

1. **Do they already have a CFO?** On the company's LinkedIn page, open the People tab and search "CFO" and "finance". If a CFO or VP of Finance is listed, move on.
2. **Do they fit your ideal client?** Check industry, size and location against the clients you serve best. A short list of good fits beats a long list of maybes.
3. **Who decides?** At companies under about $50 million in revenue, write to the owner, CEO or president. They feel the pain, and they make the decision. Their name is usually on the website's About page or on LinkedIn.

## How to follow up without being a pest

Many owners won't reply to the first message. That's usually timing, not a no. Follow up twice, about a week apart, and add something useful each time.

**Follow-up 1 (about a week later):** offer something specific and useful, tied to the signal. Here's an example for signal 1:

> Hi [Name], one more thought. A new controller will get the monthly close in shape, but most owners still can't see cash more than a few weeks out. A 13-week cash forecast fixes that. Happy to send the template I use, no strings attached.

**Follow-up 2 (about a week after that):** make it easy to say "not now".

> Hi [Name], I'll leave it here. If cash forecasting or the bank relationship becomes a priority later this year, I'd be glad to help. Good luck with the new hire.

Then stop. Log the company and check back if a new signal appears.

## Rules that keep it from feeling like spam

- **Lead with the signal.** Your first line should show you noticed something specific about their business.
- **Move within two weeks.** Timing is the whole advantage.
- **Write to one person at a time.** Three to five personal messages a week will do more than three hundred generic ones.
- **Say clearly who you are, and stop if they ask.** If you're sending email in the US, the CAN-SPAM Act also requires your postal address and a clear way to opt out.

## The 30-minute weekly routine

**One-time setup (about 15 minutes):**

- Save the job searches from signals 1 and 2, with alerts turned on.
- Create the two Google Alerts from signal 3.
- Bookmark the EDGAR search from signal 5.
- Start a spreadsheet with these columns: company, signal, date seen, contact, first message date, follow-up dates, outcome.

**Every week, same day (30 minutes):**

1. **Scan (10 minutes).** Check your job alerts, Google Alerts and new Form D filings.
2. **Qualify (10 minutes).** Run the two-minute check on anything promising.
3. **Reach out (10 minutes).** Send three to five first messages, plus any follow-ups that are due, and log them.

## The hard part is consistency

None of this is complicated. The hard part is doing it every week. Thirty minutes sounds easy until a client deadline lands. Outreach is the first thing to slip, follow-ups go cold, and you're back to waiting on referrals.
